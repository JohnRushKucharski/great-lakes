## 1. Purpose and bottom line

This memo assesses how resilient the planned habitat restoration in Dead Horse Bay, the marshy area behind Longtail Point, is to plausible future lake level changes. The plans include planted emergent and submergent marsh zones and woody debris structures designed as habitat for musky fish. We ask a simple question: if lake levels change as the GLRI scenarios indicate they can, will the planned elevations still provide the intended habitat?

We answer this question in three ways First, we map where marsh zones and structures sit relative to future water depths. Second, under each scenario we evaluate habitat reliability by counting how many years each elevation would support the intended habitat. Third we evaluate how lake level variability, i.e. the natural high- and low-water level swings, could change under the lake level scenarios. The results identify which elevations are robust, meaning they work across the near- and moderate-term scenarios. They also show where the plan is brittle, for example the scenarios under which habitat structures may be left perched above the water.

The memo builds from a quick screen to a species-level assessment. Other restoration projects can follow the same steps with the same lake-level data. This analysis provides limited, conditional guidance on restoration and conservation efforts under future lake level changes. It does not make a recommendation. Important design considerations (e.g., cost, habitat benefits, engineering tradeoffs, etc.) that would need to be incorporated to arrive at a recommendation are outside the scope of this analysis.

**Bottom line.**

- **Planting elevations:** The surveyed planting area is robust through the scenarios with 1.5 Celsius (C) warming, but at 3 C lake levels can rise or fall, and the habitat zones that survive differ. Submergent (pondweed) bottom elevations of 571 to 573 ft remain suitable under the baseline, both 1.5 C scenarios and part of 3 C (tier: 1.5C + part of 3C).

- **Woody debris depth and placement:** The structures support juvenile and spawning habitat, under on the median May-August water level, through both 1.5 C scenarios.

However, lake levels can vary within any given scenario. Therefore, we also assess how reliably the structures provide juvenile and spawning habitat under each scenario, using a target of suitability of at least 75% of years. Under the wet 1.5C scenario (a 5% increase in average annual precipitation), the shallow structure, located at approximately 576 ft, meets the 75% reliability target. The deeper structure, located at about 575 ft, meets these  target under the drier 1.5C scenario (a 0% change in precipitation), but none of the structures meet these targets under both scenarios.

No single bottom elevation is robust. Meaning that no single bottom elevation is able to meet the reliability targets across all six 1.5 C and 3 C scenarios. Together the structures are robust across most of the 1.5 C and 3 C scenarios, but under the 3 C dry scenario (i.e., 3C/0) one end is perched, and under the 3C wet scenario (i.e., 3C/15) the structures are too deep to be reliable spawning habitat. Recruitment zones are only supported under lower lake level scenarios.

- **Plant palette:** Planting palettes that span multiple marsh zones are more robust, because the wettest (i.e., 3C/15) and driest (i.e., 3C/0) scenarios favor opposite zones (i.e., shoreline and shallow marsh habitat emerge under dry scenarios, submergent habitat expands under wet scenarios).

- **Depth robustness: brittle at the extremes.** No bottom elevation is reliable across all four 3 C scenarios for either submergent vegetation (i.e., pondweed) or musky habitat. Widening the planting domain upslope and downslope is the main way to hedge.

These are conditional findings based on average monthly lake levels.

## 2. Project context

**Setting.** Longtail Point (LTP) is a sand spit on the Lake Michigan shoreline near Green Bay, Wisconsin (Suamico, Brown County). The spit is habitat in its own right and shelters Dead Horse Bay behind it. Lake Michigan and Lake Huron are hydraulically connected lakes, so the combined Michigan-Huron water level data drives this analysis.

**Goal.** The goal of the LTP project is marsh habitat restoration and conservation in Dead Horse Bay. The project delivery team plans two kinds of work:

1. Restore a range of marsh habitats through plantings: emergent marsh near shore and submergent (underwater) vegetation in deeper water.

2. Build woody debris structures to support native musky spawning, recruitment, and the juvenile stage. The 35% preliminary plan (Ducks Unlimited, 20 August 2026; in cooperation with WDNR and NOAA) calls them wood break walls: groups of cabled logs, root wads, and log piles, with slash packed between the logs and boulders as ballast. The plan sets the lake bed under the structures at an average of about 575.0 to 577.1 ft and marks three reference levels: High Water (HW) 581.95 ft, Average water (AW) 579.94 ft, and Low Water (LWD) 576.15 ft. [Review: Jenny: confirm what HW, AW, and LWD stand for and the datum of the plan elevations; see Section 3.]

**Future lake levels.** The climate of the Great Lakes has trended warmer and wetter in recent decades: the regional average annual air temperature rose about 2.6 F (1.4 C) and annual precipitation increase by about 14% from 1951 to 2023, and this trend has accelerated in recent years. Long-term lake levels are the result of a balance between evaporative losses that increase in a warmer climate, and precipitation driven gains. Increases in average annual precipitation can therefore raise, lower or leave lake levels unchanged.

The 17 lake levels evaluated in this memo are comprised of plausible combinations of warming and precipitation change. These scenarios can be motived by the observational changes described above. They can also be motivated by climate projections. All current emission scenarios trend toward 3C warming in the Great Lakes within the next few decades. 5 to 7 C warming in the Great Lakes region is possible after the mid-century, but these scenarios depend heavily on the trajectory of future emissions.

This analysis focuses on 1.5 to 3C warming scenarios. These are near-to-mid-term scenarios that are likely to be more relevant over restoration planning efforts time horizon. 5 to 7 C scenarios are provided as additional context, and are most relevant to future adaptive planning efforts.

**This analysis.** Plantings and structures are fixed in place, but lake levels are not. Design decisions made now must continue to work over the near to medium term, with little intervention. The decisions this memo informs are:

- planting elevations;

- woody debris depth and placement;

- the plant palette, including transitions between marsh types; and

- how robust the chosen depths are to lake-level change.

We assume the structures will not be moved or added to later, and the failure most important to avoid is a structure left perched out of the water.

The lake-level scenarios used in this analysis were created with funding from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Great Lakes Restoration Initiative (GLRI). Because the same data are available lake-wide, the memo doubles as a worked example: it moves from a quick screen to a species-level assessment so that other restoration projects can follow the same steps.

## 3. Inputs

**Lake-level scenarios.** The GLRI scenarios are 1,030-year monthly series of lake-wide average (AVG) Lake Michigan-Huron water levels for 17 lake level scenarios comprised of combinations of warming and precipitation change. They are the output of synthetic temperature and precipitation records run through NOAA's Net Basin Supply, Large Lake Thermodynamics, and Great Lakes operations models. The combinations span a plausible range, based on literature review, historical trends, projected trends, and thermodynamic theory.

**Scenario subset.** The analysis focuses on the *robust set*, a subset that for this analysis includes the baseline and six near- (1.5 C) and moderate-term (3 C) scenarios: two at 1.5 C of warming (precipitation +0% and +5%) and four at 3 C (+0%, +5%, +10%, and +15% precipitation change).

Scenario codes read warming (C) / precipitation change (%), for example 3C/15. Within a warming level, the *dry* scenario has +0% precipitation (3C dry = 3C/0) and the *wet* scenario has the largest precipitation increase (3C wet = 3C/15). The text says dry and wet; tables and figures use the codes. The 5 C and 7 C scenarios are long-term context and for this analysis support stress tests only.

**Site elevations.** DU surveyed water depths at 288 points across the proposed restoration area in 2016. The simulated baseline annual average level, 579.16 ft, is the datum for turning each survey depth into a bottom elevation: bottom elevation = 579.16 ft minus surveyed depth. Depth in any scenario is then that scenario's water level minus the bottom elevation. Woody debris bottom elevations come from the survey depths nearest each end of the three structures (575.01 to 576.46 ft). The plan's reference level LWD, 576.15 ft, sits within this range. [Review: Jenny: confirm the six bottom elevations still match the 35% plan layout.]

**Datum and units.** The lake-level source data are in meters, IGLD85. The analyses here use IGLD85, with the site bottom elevations in feet and the scenario levels converted from meters to feet. No datum conversion is applied. The elevations on the 35% plan appear to be NAVD88 (its AW of 579.94 ft equals 579.50 ft IGLD85 in the project's comparison of datums). [Review: Jenny: confirm the plan datum, and how the plan's 576.15 ft is to be compared with the IGLD85 bottom elevations.]

**Water level data limitations.** AVG levels are monthly averages of the lake as a whole. They do not include storm surge, wave set-up, etc. A separate GLRI storm water level (SWL) dataset provides storm-driven frequency curves, including storm surge and wave set-up, but is not used in this memo.

## 4. Two lenses

In this analysis the same lake-level data are read in two ways.

**Lens A: depth at average levels.** Where do the planted zones and structures sit relative to the average/median water level in each scenario? To answer this question, we subtract the bottom elevation from the annual average water level (for marsh zones) or the May-August median water level (for woody debris structures). We then compare the resulting depth with the depth range required for each marsh zone or habitat structure. This answers: does each part of the plan still sit in the intended zone? It is quick and easy to read on a map, but it uses one typical level per scenario.

**Lens B: yearly suitability and variability .** Lens A hides the interannual variability under each lake level scenario. Lens B evaluates suitability under the range of simulated years. For each bottom elevation and each scenario, we test whether water depths meet the pre-defined habitat requirements in each simulated year. The share of years that meet these requirements is the *reliability*. A bottom elevation is *reliable* if it provides suitable habitat in at least 75% of the modeled years under any given scenario; it is *marginal* if it meets the habitat targets in 50 to 75% of the modeled years, and *unreliable* if it meets the habitat targets in less than 50%.

*Pondweed* habitat (the submergent marker) needs 1 to 3 m of water in the growing season (May-August), at least 0.5 m in winter, and a rise of no more than 0.15 m per month.

*Musky* habitat needs the depth ranges listed in the Appendix. These include requirements for spawning, juvenile, and recruitment habitat. Recruitment habitat is not intended to exist every year. For recruitment, we assess the share of 5-year windows containing at least 3 years of suitable habitat, using the thresholds in the Appendix. Lens B also shows whether the lake keeps the low- and high-water swings the habitat is adapted to.

Results are presented in four levels of increasing specificity, from a quick screen to a species-level assessment. Lens A informs Levels 1 to 3; Lens B informs Levels 3 and 4 and the robustness chart. Each level explains when to use it.

## 5. Results in four levels

The analysis and results are presented in four levels of increasing specificity.

**Level 1. Quick screen: Lake level scenarios**

*When to use this level:* as a first screen, before any site-specific data are available or needed. It shows how far the lake could move and a plausible range of lake levels. Any restoration project on Lake Michigan-Huron can reuse it as is.

**Observed trends.** The Great Lakes region has already warmed about 1.4 C (2.6 F) and become about 14% wetter between 1951 and 2023. The region is warming faster than the global average. Warming alone lowers lake levels, due to warming-driven increases in evaporation. Increasing precipitation can raise lake levels, if the increase exceeds the evaporative loss. Which effect wins depends on this balance between precipitation and evaporation, so wetter climates can increase, decrease or leave lake levels unchanged. Figure 1 shows that balance across the scenarios. Warming is the better-constrained variable; precipitation changes are far less certain.

**Plausible change by scenario.** Compared with the baseline average level (579.16 ft), the mean level moves by:

- 1.5 C (near term): -1.6 ft (1.5C/0) to +0.2 ft (1.5C/5);

- 3 C (moderate term): -3.1 ft (3C dry) to +1.9 ft (3C wet), with 3C/5 at -1.3 ft and 3C/10 at +0.2 ft; and

- 5 C and 7 C (long term, context only): as low as -7.3 ft, and no higher than about +2 ft.

**Level 3. Specific design: woody debris and musky**

*When to use this level:* to judge a specific design—in this case, the three woody debris structures (six ends) intended to provide musky habitat. Figure 6 is the quick check at typical levels (Lens A); Figure 7 shows how often each bottom elevation works (Lens B).

**How often each bottom elevation works (Figure 7).** Reliability is expressed in percentage of years under each scenario in which the habitat requirements  are met. This changes the picture in three ways.

**1. Recruitment is a shallow-water outcome.** Recruitment zones lie shoreward of the spawning zones and provide additional spawning habitat in the years in which they are flooded. Recruitment is therefore unreliable at deeper bottom elevations, as expected. Only bottom elevations near 577 to 579 ft reach the recruitment threshold in baseline-like years; in the dry scenarios the reliable range moves down to 574 to 576 ft.

**2. Spawning and juvenile habitat move with the lake.** At 576 ft, juvenile and spawning habitat are reliable in the baseline, and more moderate 1.5C/5, 3C/5, and 3C/10 scenarios, but not in the drier, 1.5C/0, 3C/0; or wetter, 3C/15 scenario. For example, juvenile habitat reliability falls to 70% in 1.5C/0, and spawning habitat reliability falls to 33% in 3C/15. At 575 ft, spawning and juvenile habitat are only reliable in 1.5C/0 and 3C/5 scenarios. At 573 to 574 ft, juvenile habitat is reliable in every scenario, but spawning habitat, which cannot be more than 4.92 ft [1.5 m] deep, is reliable only at some elevations under the dry scenarios, 1.5C/0 and 3C/0.

**3. Pondweed prefers the deeper bottom elevations.** Growing-season habitat is reliable at 571 to 573 ft through baseline and both 1.5 C scenarios, and in three of the four 3 C scenarios (it fails under the wettest, 3C/15 scenario at 571 ft, and under the 3C/0 scenario at 573 ft). Winter survival is reliable at 571 to 572 ft in every robust-set scenario. From 574 ft up, 1.5C/0 and 3C/0 are marginal or fail.

**4. Woody debris structures: no single bottom elevation covers the robust set.** Within the woody debris structures’ bottom-elevation range (575 to 576.5 ft), the most suitable elevation differs by scenario. Under mean water levels no single structure is robust across all the 1.5 and 3 C scenarios, but the set of three structures taken as a whole are. In other words the structures complement each other. The structures together provide reliable habitat in most of the robust set (set of 1.5 and 3 C scenarios). They do not provide reliable habitat under the 3 C dry scenario (3C/0), where the bottom elevations sit too high, or the 3 C wet scenario (3C/15), where they sit too low. **Level 4. Species and variability**

*When to use this level:* to judge species-level robustness across bottom elevations and scenarios, and to see whether the lake keeps its natural high- and low-water swings. It builds on Lens B.

**Robustness by bottom elevation (Figure 10).** The habitats of musky and pondweed (used as submergent habitat indicator species) are assessed for separate aims. Each is judged independently, and a bottom elevation serving both aims is a bonus. Each cell shows the widest group of scenarios in which a bottom elevation is reliable, centered on the baseline. For each aim, the weakest habitat component sets the result (musky: juvenile and spawning, with recruitment shown separately; pondweed: growing season and winter survival).

- **Pondweed:** bottom elevations of 571 to 573 ft hold through baseline, both 1.5 C scenarios, and part of 3 C. No bottom elevation holds through all four 3 C scenarios, because 3C/0 favors the deeper ones and 3C/15 the shallower ones.

- **Musky (juvenile and spawning):** bottom elevations of 576 to 577 ft hold at baseline and one 1.5 C scenario (1.5C/5). 573 to 575 ft work only when the lake is lower. 578 to 579 ft work only when the lake is higher.

- **Both aims:** no bottom elevation serves both beyond these partial tiers. Because each aim stands alone, this is not a failure of the plan.

**Hydrograph and cycles (Figures 8 and 9).** The lake does not sit at one level. It swings over multi-year cycles, and the habitat is adapted to those swings. Figure 8 shows 200 years of monthly levels and the 12-month mean for the baseline, 3C/0, and 3C/15. The swings have the same shape in all three scenarios, but the whole record shifts down in 3C/0 and up in 3C/15. Figure 9 counts how often the 12-month mean enters each kind of cycle:

- **Low-water cycle:** the 12-month mean stays between 577.1 and 578.4 ft (175.90 to 176.30 m) for 24 to 60 months.

- **High-water cycle:** the 12-month mean stays at or above about 579.9 ft (176.75 m) for at least 24 months.

Under baseline conditions, there are 3.6 low-water and 4.4 high-water cycles per 100 years, which is broadly consistent with the baseline frequencies (see Appendix for more discussion of this topic). In the robust set:

- **Dry scenarios lose the high-water cycle.** 1.5C/0 has 0.5 high-water cycles per 100 years and 3C/0 has none. 1.5C/0 has more low-water cycles (5.7), and 3C/5 has 6.5. 3C/0 has only 2.6 because the lake spends long periods below the low-water band rather than in it.

- **Wet scenarios lose the low-water cycle.** 3C/15 has 0.1 low-water cycles and 5.3 high-water cycles per 100 years.

- **Both swings survive near baseline.** 1.5C/5 (3.3 low, 4.8 high) and 3C/10 (3.3 low, 4.9 high) keep both cycles at about the baseline rate.

- **Stress tests.** Under 5 C and 7 C most scenarios lose one or both cycles.

Retention of natural low- and high-water swings therefore depends on the balance between precipitation and warming-driven evaporative losses. The cycles are an added check on the lake's variability, not a habitat target. A cycle count also depends on the band and threshold chosen.

## 6. Options and advice

The options below are conditional advice, not a recommendation. Cost, habitat benefit, engineering tradeoffs, etc. are outside the scope of this analysis.

1. **Plant for 1.5 to 3 C, not for the baseline.** Under 1.5C/0 the whole domain shifts about 1.6 ft downslope, and under 3 C it shifts -3 to +2 ft. Choosing planting elevations that suit the baseline only would leave them brittle.

2. **Widen the planting domain upslope and downslope.** This keeps every marsh zone represented whether the lake drops (3C dry) or rises (3C wet).

3. **Use a palette that spans zones.** Mixing shallow emergent, rooted floating, and submergent species lets the community sort itself along the depth gradient as the lake moves.

4. **Woody debris structures as planned.** The analysis found no single bottom elevation reliable across the robust set, but the structures complement each other: the shallow structure (3) at about 576 ft serves baseline-like levels and the deeper structure (1) at about 575 ft serves 1.5C/0. If the lake tracks 3C dry or 3C wet, they would not be enough, and adding or moving structures would be the adaptation.

5. **Treat 5 C and 7 C as stress tests.** They show where perching becomes general, but they are not design targets.

**Next steps.** Monitoring the bay's actual water levels and vegetation response would show which scenario the lake is tracking. That would support adaptive management, such as the structure changes in option 4. This memo does not recommend a specific monitoring or management plan, only that such a plan be considered.

The check is that the definitions give counts of the same order as the historical record.

The historical low-water cycles began in 1923, 1957, 1965, 1999, 2004 and 2008, and the high-water cycles in 1952, 1971, 1983 and 2017. **A6. How to read response surface plots.** Figures 1 and 8 are response surface plots: these show the performance across the range of scenarios, with each filled square or pixel showing how the lake performs under one scenario.

- **Horizontal axis:** change in precipitation over the Great Lakes region, from +0% on the left to +20% on the right.

- **Vertical axis:** warming, from +0 C at the bottom to +7 C at the top.

- **Lower left square:** the baseline, with no warming and no change in precipitation. Every other filled square is one of the other 16 lake level scenarios, a plausible combination of warming and precipitation change. Together with the baseline, these are the 17 scenarios.

- **Number and color:** the number in each square is the result for that scenario. For example, in Figure 1, it is the change in average lake level under each scenario. The color shows how that result compares with the baseline. For example, in Figure 1, grey indicates no change from baseline, red indicates lower than baseline lake levels, and blue indicates higher than baseline lake levels. The darker the color, the larger the difference.

- **White/unfilled squares:** combinations of warming and precipitation (i.e., large precipitation increases with little corresponding warming) that were judged to be unlikely (i.e., mostly the lower right) and were not evaluated.

Moving up a grid shows the effect of more warming, and moving right shows the effect of more precipitation. Color changes across the grid show how sensitive the result is to warming and precipitation.
